Why this is proof

The two columns are independent: one is what the application renders to a shopper, the other is what the database contains. They can be read against each other cell by cell, and every cell agrees — six sizes for each of two garments, both prices, and both totals.

The two disagreements that would matter most are the ones the screenshot makes easiest to see. XS on the Raglan and XL on the Bowl tee are zero in the database, and both appear struck through rather than merely absent — the shop is not hiding what it cannot sell. XL on the Raglan holds 2, and the page says only 2 left rather than in stock, so a true count is not being rounded into a comfortable one.

2

Search-result cards appearing on the page after a chat question

passed

Asked the assistant for a kind of garment with constraints attached, and watched whether the shop page itself rearranged — or whether the answer arrived only as text inside the chat window.

What the screenshot shows

  • Across the top — three filter chips, each with a dismiss cross: “navy shirt”, navy and size M. The conversation has been turned into a visible, editable filter rather than a one-off query.
  • The grid below — six product cards the filter produced, each with photograph, name, price and the opening of its description: 2025 Yale Vs Harvard T Shirt $32, Baseball Left Chest Crewneck $58, Basic Hoodie Big Yale $68, Boola Boola T Shirt $32, Brooks Brothers Double Knit Full Zip Hoodie Yale $88, and Champion Full Zip Hood $88. Two carry stock warnings — 2 of 6 sizes sold out and 1 of 6 sizes sold out.
  • The chat panel, right — the shopper's message fine graphics are fine, and the reply: Navy garments in men's M include: followed by five named garments with prices, closing All have M in stock.
  • At the foot of the panel — a single compact row: four thumbnails, a +2, the label 6 garments · “navy shirt” · navy …, and beneath it showing on the page.

Why this is proof

The garments are on the page, not in the chat. The reply is four lines of prose; the merchandise is six full cards in the grid behind it, which is where a shopper browses. The chat did not paste a gallery into the conversation — it put the result where the shop lives.

The chips show the question was parsed into structured filters, not matched as a string. Colour and size became separate, separately removable clauses. A shopper can now take size M off by hand without going back to the assistant, which is the behaviour of a control panel rather than a search box.

The reply names five garments while the page shows six, and says include rather than listing them all. That is the intended division of labour: the cards enumerate, and the assistant says only the part a picture cannot carry.

3

Re-displaying a result from earlier in the chat

passed

Ran several different searches in one conversation, scrolled back up the chat, and clicked an earlier result to put that set of garments back on the page.

What the screenshot shows

Two moments from the same session, stacked.

  1. The upper capture — a later search in force. One chip reads under $40, and the grid holds six $32 T-shirts: 2025 Yale Vs Harvard, Benjamin Franklin, Big Yale Tri Blend, Boola Boola, District Tri Blend Vintage Shield and Football Left Chest. The chat above shows the exchange that produced it — the assistant first answering Nothing in the catalog is priced under $30, the shopper then asking okay can you display everything under $40?, and the reply explaining that there are more than six and the panel is showing the first six. The result row at the foot reads 6 garments · under $40 / showing on the page.
  2. The lower capture — an earlier result brought back. The page now reads 1 garment, and the chips have changed with it: “Yale Bowl vintage collegiate stadium graphic university merch”, ivory, under $30. A single card is on the page — the Yale Bowl T Shirt at $32, marked 1 of 6 sizes sold out. In the chat, that older exchange is visible again, and its row is the one now labelled showing on the page.

Why this is proof

The two captures show different results on the same page, and the second is not a fresh search — it is an earlier one restored. Everything that defined it came back together: its count, its single card, and all three of its original filter chips, including the unusual long query string that no one would retype.

The showing on the page marker moved. In the upper capture it sits on the under $40 row; in the lower it sits on the Yale Bowl row. The conversation keeps a record of every result and knows which one is currently in view, so a shopper can tell where they are among several.

The restored state is also still honest. Within it the assistant says plainly that nothing matches under $30 and that the shirt is $32 — a restored result reproduces what was found, not a more flattering version of it.

One observation, not a defect. In the third screenshot the Benjamin Franklin T Shirt carries the description “Campus Customs product photo (benjamin franklin t shirt). Vision blocked; filename-based stub.” That text was checked against the database and is stored in the supplied catalogue table — a placeholder in the course's own seed data, not something the application wrote. The shop is displaying it verbatim, which is the intended behaviour: descriptions are rendered exactly as stored and never rewritten.
